## Background: Von Neumann Poker

Real poker involves many strategic dimensions—position, hand types, pot odds, and countless decision points—that make formal analysis extremely difficult. Simplified poker models address this by abstracting away details while preserving core strategic elements. By isolating specific phenomena like bluffing, value betting, and bet sizing, we can derive exact Nash equilibria and develop formal understanding of poker strategy.

One fundamental simplified model is **Continuous Poker**, also called **Von Neumann Poker** (introduced by John von Neumann). The setup is minimal:

- Two players: a bettor and a caller
- Each gets a hand strength uniformly distributed between 0 and 1 (no card suits, just a number)
- One betting round
- The bettor can either check (no bet) or bet a **fixed amount B**
- The caller either calls (matches the bet) or folds (gives up the pot)
- Highest hand strength wins

In the classical **Fixed-Bet Continuous Poker** (FBCP), the bettor's bet size is a parameter $B$ that defines the game. For any given $B$, we can analytically solve for a Nash equilibrium (not uniquely, unless we impose some other constraints - but that's outside the scope of this post).

In equilibrium, the bettor divides their hands into three groups:

1. **Bluffing hands** (weak hands): Hands below threshold $\frac{B}{(1+2B)(2+B)}$ that bet hoping to make the opponent fold
2. **Checking hands** (medium hands): Hands which hope to win at showdown, but are not strong enough to extract extra value from betting
3. **Value betting hands** (strong hands): Hands above threshold $\frac{1 + 4B + 2B^2}{(1+2B)(2+B)}$ that bet hoping to be called

The caller responds with a simple threshold: call if your hand strength exceeds $\frac{B(3 +2B)}{(1+2B)(2+B)}$.

The bettor's value under optimal play depends on $B$ and is maximized when $B = 1$ (betting size is exactly the pot size). The value is:

$$V_{FB}(B) = \frac{B}{2(1+2B)(2+B)}$$

This closed-form expression shows how bet size affects the bettor's advantage.

### No-Limit Continuous Poker

Bet sizing is a critical aspect of real poker which FBCP fails to capture. This leads us to **No-Limit Continuous Poker** (NLCP), where the bettor can choose any positive bet size after seeing their hand.

Surprisingly, the Nash equilibrium is still solvable, despite unconstrained bet sizes. In equilibrium, the bettor similarly divides their hands into bluffing, checking, and value betting groups. The surprising result is that the bettor uses exactly two hand strengths for each bet size - one bluffing and one value betting. This seems to reveal too much information, since the caller can limit the bettor's hand strength down to two possibilities after seeing a bet, but it can still be made optimal because the two hands are carefully balanced to not be exploitable.

Concretely:

- The bettor sees their hand strength $x$ and solves for the bet size $s$ such that either:
    - $x = \frac{3s+1}{7(s+1)^3}$ or $x = 1 - \frac{3}{7(s+1)^2}$
    - If no such positive $s$ exists, the bettor checks.
- The caller calls with hands $y > 1 - \frac{6}{7(s+1)}$

NLCP is always more profitable for the bettor than FBCP with any fixed bet size, since they get strictly more options. The value is:
$$V_{NL} = \frac{1}{14}$$

Answering where the constant $1/14$ comes from is more involved and not at all obvious, but makes more sense once we generalize the model later.

### Limit Continuous Poker

These two models represent endpoints on a spectrum. FBCP fixes the bet size; NLCP allows any positive bet. A natural intermediate case is to let the bettor choose a bet size within bounds: anything between $L$ (lower) and $U$ (upper). This could be used to model finite stack sizes and minimum raises in real poker, which play a significant role in strategy but are not well understood past simple cases.

This motivates us to study a new variant called **Limit Continuous Poker** (LCP) which generalizes the previous two models:

- When $L = U = B$, LCP reduces to FBCP
- When $U \to \infty$ and $L=0$, LCP approaches NLCP
- For intermediate $(L, U)$, LCP (hopefully) models something in-between

By analyzing LCP across all $(L, U)$ pairs, we can understand how betting flexibility affects optimal strategy and game value. How much does flexibility gain for the bettor? How does the caller adjust to variable bets? Also, does the optimal strategy actually approach those of the limiting games as the parameters vary?


## Main Results

### 1. Nash Equilibrium Strategy Profile

A Nash equilibrium can be found by guessing and parameterizing a reasonable structure, then setting up a system of indifference equations. This approach initially results in a system far too ugly to solve by hand but tractable for a symbolic solver like Mathematica. It turns out that the solution has a simple form, but that reducing to this form requires an extremely unintuitive change of variables, which we will get to later.

Visually, the Nash equilibrium is a generalization of the previous two cases, as expected:

### 2. Game Value

Solving for the value under optimal play was an extremely complex process involving integrating 20+ regions of a piecewise function, and there is no a-priori guarantee that this would even be analytically possible. However, simplifying the resulting expression in Mathematica and using various simplification tricks yeilds a surprisingly elegant closed-form solution as a function of two new variables, $r$ and $t$:

$$V(r,t) = \frac{1 - r^3 - t^3}{14 - 2r^3 - 2t^3}$$

where $r = L/(1+L)$ and $t = 1/(1+U)$.

This reveals a beautiful symmetry property in game value, which actually inspired the change of variables:

$$V(L, U) = V(1/U, 1/L)$$

or equivalently, 

$$V(r, t) = V(t, r)$$

This means that increasing the maximum bet constraint has *exactly the same effect* as decreasing the minimum bet constraint, in a reciprocal relationship. Not only that, but the same change of variables to $r$ and $t$ drastically simplifies the Nash equilibrium, suggesting that these are really the more fundamental parameters of the game.

### 3. Counterintuitive Parameter Effects

There are most subtle results to be found in LCP, which we give a taste of here.

One such subtlety is this: increasing the upper bet limit $U$ doesn't uniformly help the bettor for all hand strengths.

How is this possible? It turns out that although the bettor benefits from increasing $U$ for the average hand, this benefit favors stronger hands disproportionately:
- **Strong hands** benefit from larger value bets
- **Intermediate hands** suffer because the caller becomes more conservative across all bet sizes
- Each value-betting hand strength has a critical threshold $U^*$ above which expected value decreases

This strategic interdependence is counterintuitive: more favorable game parameters can decrease payoffs for specific hand types because they incentivize the caller to play differently. This mirrors real poker phenomena where deeper stacks can help premium hands, but hurt hands in a more intermediate range.

This can be shown visually. First, we see that increasing $U$ always helps the bettor on average:

But if we isolate specific hand strengths and plot their expected payoffs as $U$ varies, we see that every hand actually loses value beyond some critical $U^*$:

### 4. Convergence to Classical Variants

We can also show that LCP smoothly interpolates between FBCP and NLCP:
- As $L \to B$ and $U \to B$, strategies and game value converge to FBCP
- As $L \to 0$ and $U \to \infty$, they converge to NLCP

This validates LCP as a genuine unified framework and shows some that nice continuity properties pass from parameterization to equilibrium structure.

## Implications

The equilibrium structure illuminates fundamental principles of imperfect information games:

**Information revelation through bet sizing:** Balanced strategies must mix strong and weak hands at each bet size to prevent exploitation, but need not actually randomize. A continuous type and action space allow for deterministic yet unexploitable strategies, which is a fascinating contrast to discrete games.

**Structure and Symmetry:** The elegant form of the game value as a function of $r$ and $t$ suggests that these values, rather than the obvious $L, U$ parametrization, are somehow more fundamental to the structure of the game. The symmetry $V(r, t) = V(t, r)$ especially suggests a deeper connection between these two quantities.

**Tractable complexity:** By studying a stripped-down model, we understand principles that generalize to far more complex games, without drowning in combinatorial detail.
